Janusz Kaminski photographed Saving Private Ryan (1998), and his account of the Normandy landing sequence begins with testing rather than with shooting, because he ran trials on camera speeds, on lenses and on devices that would alter the image before any of it was committed to. The decision he singles out is one of permission rather than of technique, since the crew allowed the sand and the fake blood being thrown up around the camera to land on the lens instead of protecting the glass from it, which is the exact class of thing an operator of an earlier generation would have called out as a ruined take. What that debris does is assert a physical camera standing inside the event, because a clean lens belongs to a camera that was never really there, and once an audience grants the camera a position it begins reading the frame as a point of view rather than as a composition. He describes almost none of the sequence as movie shots, saying that it is all handheld and most of the time shot with one camera, with Mitch Dubin operating and chasing the actors while he occasionally went behind a second camera himself for a few extra shots. One camera is the harder half of that, because a sequence covered from a single moving position cannot be rescued afterwards in the cutting room by a wider angle, so the limits on what a viewer is allowed to see get set on the day instead of being chosen later.
