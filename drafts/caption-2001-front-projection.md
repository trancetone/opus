The Dawn of Man sequence in 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968) was photographed on a stage against front projection, and the surviving technical account of it is Herb A. Lightman's in American Cinematographer. Stanley Kubrick wanted the background transparencies at eight by ten inches for sharpness, found that no projector existed in that format and certainly none bright enough to throw an image across ninety feet of foreground, and had one built with effects supervisor Tom Howard around a condenser pack eighteen inches thick assembled out of standard enlarger condensers. The screen was 3M Scotchlite, a retroreflective material returning roughly a hundred times the light falling on it, but only when the projector and the camera lens sit on precisely the same axis, which is also the thing that hides the join, because a performer standing in that beam exactly fits his own shadow and covers it completely. Three still crews worked in Southwest Africa for months with eight by ten view cameras to produce the plates, Douglas Trumbull disguised the screen seams by cutting the Scotchlite into irregular pieces so the joins would read as cloud, and because the screen filled an entire wall the sets were built on a rotating platform so that angles could change without anyone moving it. Sources disagree on the screen's dimensions, variously ninety by forty feet and a hundred and ten feet wide.
