William A. Fraker operated the camera for Conrad Hall for five years, and his account of the period is that a flare was a defect to be reported, so that if the sun caught the lens the operator called it out and the take was cut. Fraker's claim for that stretch of work is that he and Hall were part of making such things admissible to studio heads and to audiences, not by accident but deliberately, by putting them into the picture on purpose. He lists what would previously have cost a cinematographer the job: frames too dark to read, a face with its eyes lost, a background burning out, a window blown to white, none of which anyone would have dared deliver. Hall would light a room so sparely that you could barely see it and still see it, and Fraker's summary is that safe was never the word with him, which describes a working method rather than a temperament.
