Margaret Booth, who ran the cutting rooms at MGM as supervising editor for roughly three decades, was by the account of the editors who worked under her the most feared person at the studio, to the point that a summons to her room was received as evidence of having done something wrong. Her instruction to them was that the pace of a picture was the editor's responsibility and so was getting the best performance out of the actors, which locates both rhythm and acting in a department usually described as assembling what other people produced. On continuity she was blunter than current practice allows, telling editors that if a cut felt right at a given frame they should make it whether or not it matched, because a cut made for the emotion will carry a great deal that a technically correct one will not. Editors who objected that a shot did not match were told she did not care, which is worth setting beside the effort the craft now spends hiding exactly those mismatches.
