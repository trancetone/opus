The clouds in Close Encounters of the Third Kind (1977) were made inside a glass tank of water divided into two layers of differing density, and the whole method rests on the fact that the boundary between those layers is invisible to a camera while acting as a hard floor to anything floating above it. Salt water goes in first and is left to settle, a thin plastic sheet is laid across its surface so that fresh water can be poured on top without the two bodies mixing, and the sheet is then drawn away, which leaves a stable interface sitting somewhere in the middle of the tank. White paint injected gently near that interface spreads along it instead of sinking through, which gives the mass a flat base and lets it billow upward into the fresh water above, and that flat base is the thing that makes the result read as a cloud rather than as ink dropped into a glass. The tank built for the film is usually described as seven feet square and four feet deep, although the reported dimensions and the paint details both vary between accounts. Credit for the particular setup is genuinely contested, with Guinness recording Scott Squires working under Douglas Trumbull's supervision, popular accounts naming Trumbull as the developer of the effect, and Trumbull himself reported as crediting Squires with having thought it up.
