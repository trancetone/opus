The city visible in the distance through the window blinds in Better Call Saul is not a backing or a plate but a row of single light-emitting diodes standing on stands outside the window, and Marshall Adams, who photographed the show, describes gelling them as a deliberate mixture of sodium and mercury vapour with the occasional white one among them. The reason for putting them on individual stands rather than using one broad source is that the camera moves, so the separate points appear and disappear between the slats of the blinds as it travels, which is what produces the sensation of driving away from a city rather than of sitting in front of a picture of one. The colour choice is doing the same work at a smaller scale, because the orange of a sodium street lamp and the greenish cast of mercury vapour are what a real American city actually throws into the sky, and a row of identical white dots would read immediately as decoration. Adams also describes going back for still frames from the first desert scene in the series, shot roughly ten years earlier on 35mm, so that the colour of the new work could be dialled to match what was already on screen rather than to present taste, on the grounds that colour was the predominant character of that particular location. What is transferable here is both halves of the same discipline, which is building an environment out of a handful of correctly coloured points rather than one large approximation, and then checking the result against a reference instead of against memory.
