The travelling mattes in Mary Poppins (1964) were made by the sodium vapour process, which won Petro Vlahos, Ub Iwerks and Wadsworth E. Pohl an Academy Award the following year, and which rests on a coincidence in the physics rather than on any skill of painting. Actors were lit with ordinary white light in front of a screen lit only by sodium lamps, whose output sits in a narrow doublet at 589.3 and 589.9 nanometres, and that yellow-orange line happens to fall in the gap between the peak sensitivities of a colour negative's red and green layers, so it can be separated cleanly from everything else in the frame. A beam-splitting prism inside the camera, two pieces of glass with interference coatings laid between them, reflected that sodium band onto one strip of film as a black and white matte while transmitting the rest of the spectrum onto a second strip as the colour image, both exposed in a single pass. Disney ran the camera for roughly three decades, from Mary Poppins through to Dick Tracy, until improved bluescreen and the cost of maintaining large sodium-lit stages retired it. The prisms are now reported lost, and the often-repeated claim that only three were ever made circulates without a firm source behind it.
