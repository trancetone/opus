Jordan Cronenweth photographed Blade Runner (1982) around a plain physical requirement, that a visible shaft of light needs a medium to travel through, which made smoke the working solution and which the film's polluted future happened to justify on its own terms. He built the interiors as warm soft uplight in the foreground against hard backlight and smoke behind it, summarising the toolkit as contrast, backlight, smoke, rain and lightning, and Ridley Scott had pointed him toward Citizen Kane for the high contrast, the unusual angles and those shafts. The practical cost of the method was that smoke density can only be judged by eye, so a draft shifting it part way through a take changes the contrast of the image, which is why he called it wonderful photographically and hard to control, and noted that many people find it objectionable to work in. The replicant eye glow was achieved in the camera rather than added afterwards, using a two-way mirror at fifty percent transmission and fifty percent reflection set in front of the lens at forty-five degrees, with a light aimed into that mirror so it returned along the lens's own optical axis and into the subject's eyes, sometimes with a faint gel to tint them. The neon fixtures throughout were run on dimmers set just above the point at which they would begin to flicker.
