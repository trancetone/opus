Gregg Toland photographed Citizen Kane (1941) and wrote the method up himself in American Cinematographer that February, which makes it one of very few canonical looks with a first-hand technical account attached to it. The depth he was after, holding usable focus from eighteen inches out to over two hundred feet, meant stopping the lens far below the f/2.3 to f/3.5 most productions were working at, and that demanded light of unusual penetrating power, which he found in the twin-arc broadsides developed for Technicolor and made the backbone of his lighting. His lenses were coated with Vard Opticoat to cut glare and pass more light, he shot on Kodak Super XX as the fastest stock then available, and some of the sets ran the length of two stages at RKO-Pathé. The ceilings are the part most often missed, because most of those sets had real ceilings built deliberately lower than a room of that style would have, which forced the lighting down onto the floor except where a section was lifted out for backlight, and the muslin had the side benefit of removing microphone shadows. Not all of the famous depth is depth, and Toland's account is worth reading beside the critics who point out that several of the most quoted compositions were made with a split diopter holding two planes of focus rather than one deep field.
