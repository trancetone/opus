Network at the National Theatre put live cameras inside the staging, and the stated aim was to get at the subjective experience of Howard Beale, which split the camerawork into two kinds of looking that an audience can tell apart without being told what they are. During the news segments studio cameras hold Bryan Cranston in the fixed, composed way a broadcast holds a presenter, and the moment the production goes off air it switches to handheld cameras on gimbals, operated by Julie Rock and Chris Jackson, who introduce themselves inside the piece. The division is a grammar decision rather than an equipment one, because a locked studio camera says you are watching the programme while a camera that roams says you are standing behind it, and the switch between them tells you which side of the broadcast you are currently on. What makes it harder than film is that those handhelds are moving around the set pulling shots while the show runs, catching what the piece calls little incidental moments, with no retake available and no shot list that can anticipate where an actor will be standing on any given night. The production's own phrase for the two of them is the camera people, the cinematographers, delivering a live movie, which is a fair description of a job that means improvising coverage in front of an audience instead of planning it beforehand.
