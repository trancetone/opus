Joseph M. Wilcots photographed the Roots mini-series, and asked how to hold a two shot in which one actor has light skin and another has dark skin, his answer is that there is no balancing to be done and the only move is to get light onto the darker-skinned actor. His example is a scene in Roots: The Next Generations where James Earl Jones was the star and Johnny Sekka the guest star, and the two of them walk into the village together, which on the stock of the period would have left Sekka reading as a silhouette beside him. What he told his gaffer was to set three ten-kilowatt units and to have them follow Sekka rather than Jones, with Jones exposed normally and his level merely lifted a little, so the moving light belonged to the actor the film was least generous to. The result he reports is simply that you could see Sekka throughout the show and that he looked good in it. The convention this overturns is worth naming, because lighting to the lead and letting the rest of the frame fall where it falls sounds like a technical default and is actually a decision about who in the shot the audience is permitted to read, which makes the assignment of the three brightest units on the floor a question of billing as much as of exposure.
