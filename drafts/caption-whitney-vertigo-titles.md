The spirals in the title sequence of Vertigo (1958) were drawn by a machine built out of a World War II anti-aircraft gun director, which John Whitney acquired as war surplus and adapted because it did the one thing he needed, which was turn continuously through full revolutions without winding its own wiring into a knot. Saul Bass had designed the sequence around Lissajous curves, the figures traced by two perpendicular oscillations of differing frequency and named for the nineteenth century physicist who studied them, and the problem this created was mechanical rather than artistic, since drawing those figures by hand across the number of frames a title sequence needs was not a practical proposition. Whitney's answer was to mount the artwork on the gun director's rotating table and hang a pendulum above it carrying a pen fed from a pressurised paint reservoir set roughly twenty four feet overhead, so that the rotation of the table underneath and the swing of the pendulum above combined into the curve without anybody drawing it. Accounts of the machine differ on its configuration, several of them describing parts of the original M-5 director being replaced later with components from the more advanced M-7 to produce a rig standing around twelve feet tall. The claim that circulates most widely about this sequence, that it is the first computer animation in a feature film, is worth holding at arm's length, because the gun director was an analogue calculating mechanism rather than a computer in the sense the word now carries.
