The title sequence of Vertigo (1958) spends its opening on a face and only afterwards becomes the thing everyone remembers, travelling from a woman's lips up to her eyes, which shift one way and then the other, before the camera settles into a close-up of a single eye out of which the word VERTIGO rises. Saul Bass designed that progression along with the red wash that floods the frame as it happens, so the sequence has already carried the audience from anatomy into abstraction before any spiral appears, and the spirals then arrive as though the eye had generated them rather than having them laid over the top. Those spirals are Lissajous figures, the shapes traced when two perpendicular oscillations of differing frequency are combined, and John Whitney produced them with a machine built out of a surplus World War II anti-aircraft gun director, which he wanted for the one property he needed, that it could turn continuously through full revolutions without winding its own wiring into a knot. The artwork sat on that turning table while a pendulum swung above it carrying a pen fed from a pressurised paint reservoir set roughly twenty four feet overhead, so the rotation underneath and the swing above combined into the figure without anybody drawing it. Two parts of the usual account are worth holding loosely, since sources disagree on whether the face belongs to Kim Novak or to an uncredited model, and the claim that this is the first computer animation in a feature film rests on calling an analogue calculating mechanism a computer.
