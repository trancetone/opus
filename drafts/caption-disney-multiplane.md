Walt Disney's own account of the multiplane camera, filmed in 1957 for the Disneyland television programme, begins not with the machine but with the cheat it was built to replace, a panorama in which a character walks on the spot while a single painted background slides behind him a fraction of an inch per frame. The flaw he points to is the moon, because when the whole background travels as one sheet the moon moves along with the trees and grows or shrinks with them, which is wrong in a way an audience feels without being able to diagnose it. His answer was to break the painting into separate planes ordered by their distance from the viewer, put the moon on the plane furthest back, and drive each plane at its own speed, so that the near trees sweep past while the moon holds still and keeps its proper size, with the camera mounted above the stack and shooting downward through it rather than sideways. Disney describes the rig in the first person as a super cartoon camera and says they called it the multiplane because it used many planes, which is about as plain a naming as this industry has ever managed. Credit and paperwork are both muddier than the demonstration, because William Garity is generally named as the engineer who built the 1937 camera while the patent is variously attributed and the numbers cited for it disagree, and glass-layer rigs by Lotte Reiniger in the nineteen twenties and a horizontal one by Ub Iwerks in 1933 both came earlier.
