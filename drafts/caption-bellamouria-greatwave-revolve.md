The Great Wave at the National Theatre projects onto almost everything on stage at once, and Neville Bellamouria, the lighting control technician who programmed the video desk for that production, lists the surfaces as the Japanese screens that travel up and down, the stage floor, the revolve, and a wave structure looming over all of it. Two projectors do the work and both sit front of house, one of them very wide and covering the entire stage including the floor and the revolve and the wave, the other focused only on the wave structure, with the two images blended together where they overlap. Splitting the job that way buys resolution where the audience is actually looking, because a single projector sharp enough to hold detail on the wave would have to be throwing that same density across the whole floor as well. The part worth stealing is what drives the content, because it is programmed to track the movements of the revolve using data arriving live from the automation system that turns it. That inverts the usual arrangement, since a projected image normally sits behind a set and waits, whereas here the picture is a dependent of the machinery and knows where the stage has got to, which is the difference between scenery that is lit and scenery that is addressed.
