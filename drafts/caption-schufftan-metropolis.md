Eugen Schüfftan worked as effects cinematographer on Metropolis (1927), where the problem was that Fritz Lang wanted actors moving through architecture far larger than any set could be built, and the answer turned out to be a mirror rather than a painting. A sheet of mirrored glass stands at forty-five degrees in front of the lens and the silvering is scraped away in exactly the area where the performers are to appear, so the camera records the actors directly through that cleared patch while recording a miniature in the reflection everywhere else in the frame. The performers stand behind the mirror at a distance chosen so their scale matches the model, which means the stage and the miniature have to be sized against each other before a single frame is exposed. In the worker's city the lower floors were built full scale while the upper floors exist only as a reflected model, and the join between the two is simply the edge of the scraped area. Accounts differ on whether the plate was a mirror with its coating removed or a partly silvered glass, and the better-documented lineage runs back through Pepper's ghost on the Victorian stage and through Schüfftan's own earlier use of the method on Die Nibelungen in 1924.
