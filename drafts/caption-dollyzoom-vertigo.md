The shot looking down the mission stairwell in Vertigo (1958) is generally credited to Irmin Roberts, an uncredited second-unit cameraman at Paramount, and the method behind it is two opposed movements run simultaneously against each other. The lens zooms in one direction while the camera dollies in the other at a matched rate, so the subject holds the same size in the frame throughout while everything behind it either rushes away from the viewer or crowds forward onto them. Nothing about the subject itself changes and the entire effect lives in the background, which is why it registers as a bodily sensation rather than as a camera move, and why it has been borrowed continually since under half a dozen names including contra-zoom, trombone shot and simply the Hitchcock zoom. Two details are worth holding loosely because each rests on a single account: that the stairwell was a large model laid on its side and shot downward into, and that those few seconds cost somewhere in the region of nineteen thousand dollars. The attribution to Roberts is the accepted one rather than a documented one, and he never received a screen credit for the shot that made his method famous.
