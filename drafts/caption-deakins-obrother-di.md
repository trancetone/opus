Roger Deakins photographed O Brother, Where Art Thou? (2000) on location in Mississippi across June and July, which left the foliage lush and green while the Coens wanted a parched Dust Bowl palette drawn from hand-tinted photographs and faded postcards of the Depression. Conventional photochemical timing could not reliably suppress that green across an entire feature, and the chemical route to the look would have meant flashing the negative, sepia filtration and repeated passes through the bath, with the result locked in once the print existed. The production sent the film instead to Cinesite in Los Angeles, a Kodak subsidiary, where the negative was scanned at 2K on a Spirit DataCine, graded digitally on a Pandora MegaDef, and recorded back out to film on a Kodak Lightning II for release prints. Deakins spent roughly eleven weeks on that grade, and he approached it knowing what had been done on Pleasantville, which Cinesite had also scanned and recorded. The picture is routinely cited as the first digital intermediate applied across the whole of a first-run Hollywood feature carrying almost no visual effects, which is a narrower claim than the one usually repeated, and what it established is that colour had stopped being a property fixed at the lab and become a parameter held open until the last possible moment.
