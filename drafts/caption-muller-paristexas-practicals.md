Robby Müller photographed Paris, Texas (1984) for Wim Wenders, and the governing decision was a refusal rather than a technique, because where most cinematographers correct practical sources he kept them, his instruction as Criterion records it being that they were going to keep them all. The consequence runs through the whole picture, since neon and fluorescent tubes that a conventional job would have gelled or balanced away are left to do whatever they do on tungsten-balanced stock, which is a large part of why the film sits in a green cast, uncorrected white neon reading green on film. Wenders describes the same policy from the other side, saying that the things other people took out as mistakes they used as a virtue, and he recalls Müller pushing to stop the processing lab from correcting those colours after the fact. The interiors are built on the same economy, largely a camera on a tripod with the principals backlit or dropped into darkness while secondary figures behind them sit more clearly exposed. What is transferable here is not a lighting plan at all but a standing instruction to the laboratory, which is a cheaper decision than any of the equipment and a harder one to hold.
