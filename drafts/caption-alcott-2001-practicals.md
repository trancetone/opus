John Alcott took over the photography of 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968) when Geoffrey Unsworth, who had begun the film, could not stay to complete it and Stanley Kubrick handed the remaining work to him, which is how an operator became the cinematographer of four Kubrick pictures. Alcott says that job settled what he wanted to do, because it was the first time he had seen a picture lit to its full extent by light existing inside the set rather than by units standing outside it. The working method that followed from this is that Kubrick knew in advance which practical lamps would be carrying the scene, and framed those lamps in the shot as often as he could, down to candles and oil lamps, which is the principle Barry Lyndon (1975) was later built on. Once a lamp is both the source and part of the composition, the staging has to be arranged around where the light can plausibly sit, which inverts the usual order in which a scene is blocked and then lit.
