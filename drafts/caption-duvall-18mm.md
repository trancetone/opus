Shelley Duvall has described Stanley Kubrick's persistent use of an 18mm lens across The Shining (1980) as a standing frustration for both herself and Jack Nicholson, who came to dread the instruction as he phrased it on set, a request for the 18 in the tube, because of what a lens that wide does to a face at the distance a close-up requires. An 18mm renders rooms and furniture well, an application Duvall allows it suits, but on a face it bows the features outward from the optical centre and enlarges whatever sits nearest the front element, producing an image that reads as subtly incorrect without declaring itself manipulated.

Duvall and Nicholson asked repeatedly for a 50mm or a 75mm, and John Alcott, who photographed the film, intervened on their behalf more than once without result, Duvall's reading of the refusal being that the distortion was the intended effect rather than a cost Kubrick absorbed. Photographing a performance through a focal length that deforms it moves part of the work of unsettling an audience off the actor and onto the lens, where it operates below the threshold at which a viewer identifies the cause.
