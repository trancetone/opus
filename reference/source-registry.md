# Source Registry

Series and documentaries where practitioners narrate over their own footage.
This is the preferred source shape: primary-source analysis, footage that
demonstrates it, and speech for Opus to curate and caption from, all
pre-assembled by people who held the rights to cut them together.

## The screening test for a series

**Does footage run under the voice, or is it a person in a chair?**

A talking head cannot demonstrate anything. This single test decides whether a
series is a sourcing source or only a research source.

## The structural problem, and the one class of source that escapes it

A documentary where a practitioner's voice runs over film clips must license
those clips. That cost is why the films with the right structure are commercial
products, and why the free institutional material is mostly talking heads: a
person in a chair needs no clip rights. **Legitimacy and the required structure
pull against each other.**

The exception is an institution that owns both the films and the documentary
about them. It can use the footage freely and has no reason to withhold it.

### National Film Board of Canada

A federal agency streaming over 7,000 films free, no ads, no subscription. It
produced the work, so it can cut its own footage under its own interviews.

Norman McLaren holdings fit the visual remit directly: direct animation,
drawing onto the film strip, pixillation.

**Official channel confirmed:** `youtube.com/user/nfb` hosts full films free,
over 7,000 titles. Official NFB uploads are therefore a supported Opus source,
which `nfb.ca` is not.

| Title | Shape | Availability |
|---|---|---|
| **Creative Process: Norman McLaren** (1990, dir. Donald McWilliams) | Feature length, drawn from his private film vaults, covering pixillation and his methods | **Unconfirmed.** Signals point to a download-to-own title rather than free streaming. A YouTube copy exists but its provenance is unknown, which is the only thing that matters here. Verify on the official channel before use. |
| **Animated Motion** series | McLaren's own instructional films on tempo and movement | Unverified |
| ~~Pen Point Percussion~~ (1951) | Ruled out. Concerns drawn synthetic sound, which the visual remit excludes. | — |

McLaren sits in the avant-garde band, a 1,322 median against 2,262 for canon
narrative: viable, not the strongest category. Solving the source problem here
means optimising into the weaker subject category, which is a trade to make
knowingly.

**Provenance rule.** A copy on YouTube is not the same as an official upload.
For any institution in this registry, confirm the video sits on that
institution's own channel. The whole value of this class of source is that it
is legitimately posted; an unauthorized copy of an NFB film carries the same
exposure as an unauthorized copy of anything else.

**The generalisation is the useful part.** Look for rights-holders making
documentaries about their own catalogues, not for craft documentaries in
general. Other national archives should share the property: BFI, EYE
Filmmuseum, NFSA.

## The register test, which the other tests do not cover

Added after cancelling two scheduled posts on 2026-10-02, and it is the same
failure as the Steven Seagal clip the day before.

Everything else in this repo screens for *form* and *mechanism*: caption
sentence length, a named practitioner in first person about their own decision,
a mechanism demonstrable in the clip. A studio promotional special can pass all
of that. George Lucas on why killing Ben Kenobi forced him to invent Yoda is
first person, about his own decision, consequential, and well told. It was still
wrong for the account.

The missing question is what kind of discourse the source belongs to.

| Belongs | Does not |
|---|---|
| Craft documentary, cinematographers and editors on their own work | Studio promotional special, EPK, anniversary featurette |
| Archival oral history, guild and museum interviews | Convention panel, fan retrospective |
| Criterion-class supplement attached to a restoration | Trivia, on-set anecdote, celebrity incident |

Two diagnostics that would have caught both failures:

1. **Would the clip be interesting if the film were obscure?** The Seagal
   anecdote is only interesting because it is Seagal. The Ewok language clip is
   only interesting because it is Star Wars. Garrett Brown on the Steadicam
   would be interesting if the film were unknown, because the subject is the
   device.
2. **What is the actual draw, the method or the story?** If a reader would
   repeat it as an anecdote rather than apply it as a method, it is trivia with
   analytical prose wrapped around it. Writing it up in house style does not
   change what it is.

A note on why this is not a performance question. The Star Wars clips might well
have done good numbers; fandom travels. The decoder analysis puts subject at
roughly 1.7x against caption form at 7.8x, which is exactly the reasoning that
led here, and it is the wrong frame. Editorial point of view is not a variable to
optimise against views. Anything defended with "it will probably perform" should
be re-examined on register first.

## Why diversify off a single source

Three reasons, in increasing order of seriousness:

1. The footage starts to look the same, since one documentary has one grade,
   one interview setup, and one era of transfer.
2. The audience recognises the well. A feed visibly drawn from one film reads
   as a clip account rather than an editorial one.
3. It is a single point of failure. One rights holder, one detection profile,
   one takedown affecting everything.

Target no more than roughly one post in four from any single source.

## Gap

No verified source yet where **Freddie Young** narrates over the *Lawrence of
Arabia* mirage shot. He died in 1998 and his surviving accounts are text oral
histories, which do not help a reel. Subjects whose practitioners died before
the video-interview era will keep hitting this, and that is a reason to check
source availability early rather than after the research is done.

## The scheduling window is the fragile part, not the research

Two slots have now been lost the same way: Friday 10am and Saturday 5pm. In
both cases the clip was rendered and the caption had passed the register test.
What killed the slot was elapsed time between the export call and the
`createScheduledPost` call, and Metricool refuses a past date outright
("Publication date cannot be in the past"). There is no recovery from that.

The first time I wrote the rule as "schedule what is ready before researching
what is not", which read the failure as a priority problem. It is not. The
second loss had no research in the gap at all; the gap was a context
compaction and a run of tool reloads. Anything can fill that gap.

So the rule is about adjacency, not priority: an export and its schedule write
go out together, and when a slot is inside the next hour it gets written before
anything else happens, including before polling the sibling project.
`updateScheduledPost` can move a post that was scheduled too early. Nothing can
schedule a post into a slot that has passed.

## Search YouTube with the API, not the web

For two weeks this pipeline treated footage as the hard constraint and I told
Tony that web search cannot enumerate a channel's videos, so footage-first
sourcing was not really available. The first half of that was true. The
conclusion was wrong, because `tools/screen_source.py` already proves the
YouTube Data API is reachable through the agent proxy, and the same credential
serves `search.list` as well as `videos.list`. Searching YouTube directly
returns candidate ids with channel names attached, which is the thing web
search cannot do.

The cost difference is real: `search.list` is 100 quota units against 1 for
`videos.list`, so search, then screen the shortlist rather than screening
everything. But it replaces several web searches that were failing anyway.

Three mechanisms recorded as dead at the footage stage came back in a single
pass once searched this way, including one (Paris, Texas) where the blocking
problem was a taste call about the only sequence I could find, and the API
immediately surfaced a different scene carrying the same mechanism. Before
declaring a mechanism unusable for want of footage, search the API.

## What still cannot be checked from here

Provenance is reasoning and content is not. The API gives channel names, so
whether an upload is a rights holder or somebody's copy is a judgement that
can be made from metadata. Whether the clip actually shows the mechanism
cannot, because the video cannot be watched from this seat. When the two
pull against each other, prefer the source whose title names the thing
itself over the better-provenanced source whose content has to be assumed.

## A range is only safe when the whole source shows the mechanism

The Wizard of Oz post went out with a caption about the sepia-to-Technicolor
transition over footage that stops before the door opens. The source was not
the problem: it was titled "Entering Oz | Dorothy Steps Into Technicolor" and
ran 4m38s. The problem was that I took range 0-70, which is the sepia farmhouse
interior, and the transition sits later in the clip.

The distinction worth keeping is structural. Most sources used here show the
mechanism continuously: a candlelit card scene is candlelit all the way
through, a cloud tank compilation is cloud tank shots all the way through, a
hotel interior carries its uncorrected practicals in every shot. Any window of
those is safe. But a source built as setup and payoff, where the mechanism IS
the moment of change, has a wrong half, and a window can land entirely inside
it. Oz was the only source of seven with that shape and I took the wrong half.

So: before choosing a range, ask whether the source has a before and an after.
If it does, do not use a range at all. Find a short upload whose whole content
is the payoff and take it entire with skipSlicing, which leaves no window to
get wrong.

## Hand over preview URLs before publication, not after

Whether the footage shows the mechanism is the one check that cannot be made
from this seat, and it is cheap for Tony to make: list_clips returns a
preview_url per clip that plays in a browser. Those links belong in the message
that reports a scheduled post, not in a post-mortem after it publishes. They
are signed and expire within a day or so, so they are worth re-listing if a
check is being made later.

## The clip queue page

Preview links in chat get buried, which is how a bad clip reached publication.
The standing place for them is a published artifact:

  https://claude.ai/artifact/GT25YYPKkD55cfyNs9MQmU

It reads its rows from the artifact's own database rather than carrying them in
the page source, so the queue is refreshed with ArtifactData writes to the
`queue` collection and the page does not need republishing. One document per
Metricool post id, ordered by `slot_iso`.

Fields: slot_iso, day_label, time_label, mechanism, film, claim, risk
("check" / "verified" / "continuous"), status ("pending" / "published"),
video_url, fallback_url, caption, post_id, duration_sec, source_note.

`claim` is the point of the page: it states what the caption asserts, so the
check is a comparison rather than a guess. `risk` encodes which sources can go
wrong: "check" for a setup-then-payoff source where a window can miss the
mechanism, "verified" where a transcript or the source title settles it,
"continuous" where the mechanism is present throughout and any window holds.

Clips play from Metricool's re-hosted copy, which carries no expiry. The Opus
preview is kept as `fallback_url` for when that fails, and those are signed and
lapse in about a day. Whenever posts are scheduled, write the rows at the same
time: the queue is only useful if it is current before publication, not after.

## Caption to the clip you actually have, not the clip you planned

Tony on the Vertigo titles: the clip is good, but the spirals do not arrive for
a few seconds, so talk about the whole sequence instead. The caption had been
written about the mechanism alone, and the mechanism is only the back half of
what is on screen. The first half is a face, travelling from lips to eyes to
one eye, with a red wash flooding the frame and the title rising out of the
pupil before any spiral appears.

That is a different failure from the Oz one and worth separating. Oz was a
clip that did not contain its mechanism. This was a clip that contained more
than its caption admitted. The fix is not a new clip but a caption that covers
what the viewer sees in the order they see it, and the mechanism lands better
for having been arrived at rather than announced.

So when a caption is written before the clip is cut, read it again against the
finished clip and ask what the first ten seconds show. If the caption does not
mention that, it is describing a different video.

## Metricool post ids change on update; the uuid does not

`updateScheduledPost` returns a NEW id for the same post and leaves the uuid
alone. Anything that stores a reference to a scheduled post should store the
uuid, with the id treated as a value that goes stale. The clip queue's rows
carry both for this reason.
